Zixuan Qi

Hi, I'm Zixuan, a PhD student in mechanical engineering at Texas A&M. I work on home energy systems, from the sensors on the wall to the decision about when the heat pump runs. I build models of heat pumps and rooms, write optimization-based controllers, connect them to real thermostats, inverters, and relays, and field-test them in one of the two research houses our group runs. I also built and run the data system behind both houses. On the artificial intelligence (AI) side, I work on multimodal large language models (LLMs) that understand what occupants are doing, a foundation model for home heating and cooling data, and AI agents that take on routine engineering work.

Before Texas A&M, I spent seven years studying heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) and building energy at Chongqing University and Tongji University.

I'd rather build something that runs in a real house than something that only looks good in a paper.

Projects

Published

An LLM that sees what you're about to do

Problem
What people intend to do next, such as cooking, working out, or taking a nap, changes how warm they want the room, which appliances they run, and what they expect from the HVAC. Presence sensors can tell that someone is home but not what they are about to do, so controllers often fall back on fixed schedules.
What I did
Designed and built a system that pairs a multimodal LLM with MPC. The LLM watches camera footage and infers what the occupant intends to do next. It turns that into predictions of metabolic rate, clothing, appliance use, and window state. The MPC plans the heat pump with these predictions, an RC model of the rooms fitted from measured data, and a heat pump model fitted from catalog data.
Status
Published as first author in Advances in Applied Energy, 2026. In a simulation driven by 13 hours of real video, it cut HVAC electricity by 60% and discomfort (unmet degree-hours) by 48%, compared with the same MPC on standard U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) schedules.
Field-tested

Building thermal resilience

Problem
When the grid goes down, a home with solar and a battery has to make its stored energy last and still keep the house livable.
What I did
Built the MPC controller, set up its field test, and analyzed the results. Every 15 minutes the controller solves a MILP that plans the next 12 hours for the heat pump and other household loads, using an RC model of the house, heat pump performance curves, and the battery's charge. It runs the heat pump through the Daikin API and switches a stand-in household load with a relay on a Raspberry Pi.
Status
Field-tested for three days in one of our houses in September 2026. Paper in progress. Part of a DOE-funded project led by Slipstream.

One database for two research houses

Problem
Each house runs several data systems, each with its own format, and readings are easy to lose between them.
What I did
Built and run the pipeline that integrates the houses' sensor systems into one database: LabVIEW data acquisition with more than 200 sensors, Sol-Ark inverters, eGauge power meters, and Daikin thermostats. Readings land in InfluxDB with Grafana dashboards. I designed the LabVIEW upload, set up our own MQTT server, and wrote a watchdog that emails us when any stream stops.
Status
Running every day. Our field tests use its data.

The test houses

My field work happens in the Texas A&M Smart and Connected Homes Testbed (TAM-SCHT) on the RELLIS campus in Bryan, Texas. It has two identical 1,200 ft² houses with three bedrooms and two bathrooms each. Each house has a heat pump, solar panels, a 9.6 kWh battery, and a LabVIEW system that logs its sensors and drives load emulators that follow hourly load profiles.
